# Mars Constellation System

This document defines a navigation-first constellation framework for an intelligent, pre-tech observer standing on Mars at Olympus Mons who already knows Earth constellation names.

## Purpose

This is a Mars-surface observational system, not a replacement star catalog.

Its goal is to answer a practical question: if the observer already recognizes Earth constellations, how should those same patterns be organized and used from Mars?

The answer is not to rename the sky. The answer is to reuse the same star figures while changing their role in navigation, season-keeping, and sky interpretation.

## Core Principle

Earth and Mars are far apart by human standards, but still extremely close compared with stellar distances. For naked-eye observers, the constellations are effectively unchanged from Mars.

That means:

- `Orion` is still `Orion`
- `Scorpius` is still `Scorpius`
- `Cygnus` is still `Cygnus`

What changes is not the pattern geometry, but the structure of the sky:

- the Martian pole points to a different region
- different stars become circumpolar
- seasonal timing shifts
- Phobos and Deimos become important moving lights

## What Actually Changes On Mars

### 1. The pole region changes

For this repo, the practical north-pole region is the `Cygnus` area, especially the zone around `Deneb` and `Sadr`. This makes `Cygnus` a much more important orientation constellation on Mars than it is for many Earth observers.

At Olympus Mons, the north celestial pole stands about `18.65°` above the northern horizon. That gives the observer a stable low-north pivot for organizing the sky.

### 2. Circumpolar behavior changes

Constellations that lie close to the Martian north celestial pole may never set, or may skim the horizon without fully disappearing. On Earth, many cultures organize the sky around `Polaris` and nearby constellations. On Mars, the anchor role moves toward the `Cygnus` region and its nearby support constellations.

### 3. Seasonal timing changes

Mars has a different year length, a different sidereal rhythm relative to the solar day, and a different seasonal cycle. Familiar constellations still return in familiar arrangements, but their practical calendar role must be relearned in Martian seasons rather than borrowed directly from Earth.

### 4. The moving lights feel different

The `Sun`, `Phobos`, `Deimos`, and the planets must be kept separate from the fixed stars.

For a Martian sky culture, `Phobos` is especially important because it moves quickly and can cross the sky in ways that feel dramatically different from Earth's Moon. `Deimos` is dimmer and slower, but still belongs to the class of moving lights, not to the fixed constellation framework.

## Observer Framework

The baseline observer is:

- location: `Olympus Mons`
- latitude: `18.65°N`
- longitude: `226.2°E`

The observer framework is intentionally simple and repeatable.

### Fixed observing practice

Use one repeated observing place. Mark the horizon with stones, posts, cuts in rock, or any durable ground references. The same site matters more than precision instruments.

### Cardinal directions

Use daytime shadow tracking to establish east-west and a local north-south line. Once the directions are fixed at the observing site, keep them fixed. The night map should be built against that local horizon, not against memory.

### Angular measurement

Use body-based measures:

- finger width at arm's length
- fist width
- full hand span

These are crude, but stable enough for a pre-tech sky record if always used by the same observer or teaching tradition.

### Repeatable records

Record four things for each important constellation:

- where it rises
- where it culminates
- where it sets
- what other constellations accompany it in the same season

This produces a workable sky system even without mathematics.

## Constellation Classification

Constellations should be grouped by Martian use, not by inherited Earth habit.

### Polar-anchor constellations

Rule: constellations near the Martian north celestial pole, useful for finding stable sky rotation and northward orientation.

Primary examples:

- `Cygnus`
- `Lyra`
- `Cassiopeia`

These are the first constellations used to teach the structure of the Martian sky.

### Seasonal marker constellations

Rule: constellations whose appearance, culmination, or evening prominence is useful for dividing the Martian year into recognizable seasonal periods.

Primary examples:

- `Orion`
- `Taurus`
- `Leo`
- `Scorpius`
- `Sagittarius`

These are the calendar constellations.

### Ecliptic / zodiac constellations

Rule: constellations through which the `Sun` and planets move over time.

These are important because they help separate fixed stars from wanderers and give the observer a way to track the moving lights against a known star background.

Primary examples in this starter system:

- `Taurus`
- `Leo`
- `Scorpius`
- `Sagittarius`

### Low-horizon southern constellations

Rule: constellations that are visible from Olympus Mons but remain low and observationally fragile.

These are useful as boundary markers for the southern reach of the sky, but they are not reliable all-season anchors.

Primary example:

- `Crux`

From Olympus Mons, `Crux` is a marginal low-southern figure rather than a dominant one. It belongs in the system, but with caution.

### Utility asterisms for orientation

Rule: visually distinctive sub-patterns used for quick recognition, even if the larger constellation carries the formal name.

Examples:

- the `Northern Cross` within `Cygnus`
- `Orion's Belt`
- the `Scorpion tail` in `Scorpius`
- the `Teapot` in `Sagittarius`

These are teaching tools and fast-recognition tools.

## How To Map The Sky

The mapping method should be field-based and cumulative.

### Step 1. Fix the site

Choose one observing site and keep returning to it. Do not move the reference horizon unless forced to.

### Step 2. Mark the horizon

Lay out simple permanent markers for:

- north
- east
- south
- west
- notable rise points of major constellations

This turns the horizon into a memory aid.

### Step 3. Find the rotational center

Watch which stars circle a common point in the north. Use the `Cygnus` region to locate the Martian north-pole area. Once that center is recognized, the rest of the sky becomes easier to classify as:

- circling
- rising and setting
- low southern

### Step 4. Start with the brightest figures

Map the clearest constellations first:

- `Cygnus`
- `Lyra`
- `Cassiopeia`
- `Orion`
- `Taurus`
- `Scorpius`
- `Sagittarius`

Do not begin with the full sky. Begin with repeatable anchors.

### Step 5. Record culmination

For each major constellation, note when it reaches its highest point. Culmination is more useful than first appearance because it is easier to compare across nights and seasons.

### Step 6. Separate fixed and moving lights

If a bright object shifts position against the same constellation pattern over repeated observations, it is not part of the constellation. It belongs to the moving-light class.

This prevents confusion between:

- stars
- planets
- the `Sun`
- `Phobos`
- `Deimos`

### Step 7. Build seasonal cycles

After many observations, group constellations by when they dominate the evening sky. That seasonal cycle becomes the Martian calendar framework.

## Mars Interpretation Of Familiar Constellations

### Cygnus

On Mars, `Cygnus` is the primary northern anchor. The `Northern Cross` is especially useful because it gives the observer a strong geometric figure near the Martian pole region. This is the best starting constellation for explaining rotation around the north sky.

### Lyra

`Lyra`, anchored by `Vega`, works as a compact support constellation near the northern system. It is useful for confirming orientation once `Cygnus` has been found.

### Cassiopeia

`Cassiopeia` is a strong counterweight marker on the opposite side of the pole region from `Cygnus`. Its distinctive shape makes it a good teaching constellation for pole-centered motion.

### Orion

`Orion` remains one of the best large, unmistakable sky figures. On Mars it becomes less of a universal orientation anchor and more of a seasonal teaching constellation. `Orion's Belt` remains a fast recognition tool.

### Taurus

`Taurus` is important because it combines bright anchors with zodiac relevance. It helps link the fixed-star system to solar and planetary motion.

### Leo

`Leo` is useful as a seasonal and zodiac marker. It is clearer in the system as a calendar constellation than as a directional one.

### Scorpius

`Scorpius` is a strong warm-season marker and a useful southern figure, but not a pole anchor. It helps define the sky's southern and zodiacal character.

### Sagittarius

`Sagittarius`, especially through the `Teapot` asterism, is a practical seasonal and ecliptic reference constellation. It works well as a fixed background for observing wanderers.

### Crux

`Crux` is included as a southern low-horizon marker. From Olympus Mons it should be treated as observationally valuable but geometrically limited, because it does not dominate the sky and may remain low enough to be affected by local horizon conditions.

## Moving Lights

The moving lights must be classified separately from the constellations.

### Fixed stars

These preserve their pattern over time and define the constellation system.

### The Sun

The `Sun` determines daylight, seasons, and the path of yearly motion through the zodiac constellations, but it is not part of any fixed constellation.

### Phobos

`Phobos` is a rapid moving light. It should be treated as a wanderer with strong practical significance, but never used as a fixed map anchor.

### Deimos

`Deimos` is also a wanderer, though dimmer and slower in appearance. It should be recorded separately from constellation charts.

### Planets

Planets belong to the same moving-light class. Their behavior is meaningful only when compared against the fixed background of the ecliptic constellations.
